abstract = "Product listings on major e-commerce platforms increasingly advertise artificial intelligence, yet whether this practice serves sellers well remains open. Experimental work reports that AI labeling depresses purchase intention, while recent meta-analyses conclude that consumer responses to AI are conditional rather than uniform. What no study has examined is what happens after purchase, in functioning markets, once consumers have used the product whose capability was advertised. We begin by estimating the average association between AI-signaling language and post-purchase star ratings across 8,447 products and 1,750,116 verified reviews from the Amazon Reviews 2023 dataset; and find it to be essentially zero (+0.002 stars). But the accompanying heterogeneity statistic is 83.6%, meaning the flat average is not the absence of an effect: it is two large, opposite effects cancelling each other. We reposition our research question accordingly: under which conditions does AI language in product listings help or hurt online retailers? We find that the answer depends on category structure and price tier jointly. In homogeneous categories where AI integration is technologically coherent, AI-signaling language is associated with higher ratings. In heterogeneous categories it reverses and becomes negative under covariate balancing. The damage is sharpest at the intersection of budget pricing and category heterogeneity: each additional AI-signaling pattern on a budget product in a heterogeneous category carries a penalty of 0.358 stars (p < 0.001), a net effect of −0.395 stars in that cell. Neither condition alone produces comparable harm. The rating penalty is not the full cost: reviews alleging unmet AI claims receive 15–16% more helpful votes in heterogeneous categories, amplifying the visibility of overclaiming beyond the star rating alone. Two preregistered experiments will test causality and isolate the mechanism."
